01

Why I built it

Thermal is the thread running through most of what I've done — a 12U CubeSat control subsystem at Space Ocean, a thermal control subsystem at NASA L'Space, board-level studies at Lockheed Martin. Re-entry is where that problem gets hardest, so I wanted to take it on alone, start to finish.

I picked a blunt-body capsule deliberately: it is one of the few hypersonic cases with closed-form answers to check against, which meant I could hold my own CFD to a standard instead of trusting it. I ran the whole chain myself — geometry, mesh, solver, validation, then the Python heating and conduction models that turn a flow field into a thickness.

Where it applies: entry, descent and landing; any vehicle where aerothermal loads size the structure; and more generally, anchoring a cheap correlation to high-fidelity data where high-fidelity is affordable — which is how preliminary design actually runs.

03

CFD validation

Three independent checks across Mach 5, 7.5 and 10 before any CFD number was allowed downstream. Worst error 2.85%.

Peak wall pressure against Rayleigh Pitot, peak Cp against modified Newtonian, and freestream P₀ against isentropic theory — the last one checks the boundary conditions separately from the solution.

Cp is nearly Mach-independent on the heat shield — under 2% apart across a doubling of speed.

Orientation check. Peak pressure on the heat shield, not the nose — this caught a reversed flow direction.

Shock standoff. Total pressure drops 93.9% across the bow shock at Mach 5.

One result breaks its own bound, and I'd rather say so. The Mach 7.5 Cp reads 1.878 against a Newtonian limit of 1.826 — impossible for a steady stagnation point. It is a single-facet pressure spike from shock smearing on a coarse grid, not physics.
04

Heating & heat shield

Inflation layers would not generate on this geometry, so the boundary layer is unresolved and CFD wall heat flux is not trustworthy. Surface pressure still is. So heating comes from Fay-Riddell instead, cross-checked against Sutton-Graves, then pushed along an Allen-Eggers entry into a 1-D transient conduction model.

Two independent correlations agreeing to 0.3% at Mach 10 is what validates the implementation. The shaded band marks where CFD backs it up.

Peak flux 127.1 W/cm² at 106 s, 44 km, Mach 20.1. Three ablators sized to hold the bondline under 523 K.

Avcoat sizes thinner and still weighs 43% more. Its lower ablation temperature caps surface temperature sooner, so less heat drives inward — 3.0 cm against PICA's 4.0 cm. But at 512 kg/m³ the density wins. That trade is why low-density ablators replaced Avcoat-class materials on modern vehicles.

06

Challenges & limitations

Mach 15, attempt one. Residuals idle near 10⁻¹ for 750 iterations, then blow up to 10⁺⁸.

Attempt two, Courant 0.05. Stable but never converged — residuals bottom out and climb. Stable is not the same as converged.

Mach 15 never converged

Shock thickness scales inversely with Mach, so a shock marginally resolved at Mach 5 is under-resolved at Mach 15, and the 512k cell cap blocked the refinement that would fix it. I documented both attempts and covered Mach 15–25 analytically rather than dropping them.

No inflation layers

They generated inverted cells on this imported geometry across several attempts. That makes wall heat flux unusable — but surface pressure, set by the inviscid outer flow, is still valid. Knowing which outputs survive a bad mesh is what let the project continue.

Perfect gas above Mach 12

γ = 1.4 throughout. Real shock-layer temperature at Mach 25 would be nearer 8,000–10,000 K than the 26,258 K perfect-gas theory predicts, because dissociation absorbs the difference. That is why no CFD was attempted above Mach 12.

Preliminary sizing, not design values

The ablation model is a temperature cap with energy bookkeeping and tracks no surface recession, so thicknesses under-predict flight hardware by roughly 2×. The physics and the ranking between materials hold; the absolute numbers should not be quoted as design values.
